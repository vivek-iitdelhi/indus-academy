If your company piloted a voice bot in the last two years and it's still handling the same narrow set of calls it started with, you're not behind. You're the median. That's the real finding buried in Nasscom's new India Voice AI Readiness 2026 report, released with Krisp: most Indian organizations running voice AI are stuck exactly where they started, and the gap between "we tried it" and "it changed how we work" is wider than most leadership teams realize.

## What the 2026 numbers actually show

Nasscom and Krisp's [India Voice AI Readiness 2026 report](https://www.business-standard.com/technology/tech-news/voice-ai-adoption-scales-up-in-india-moves-beyond-experimentation-126100801453_1.html) surveyed Indian organizations on where their voice AI deployments actually stand, not where their pitch decks say they're headed. Three numbers matter most:

- **31%** of surveyed organizations have moved past early testing into limited production: voice AI handling real calls, but only in a narrow slice of the business.
- **13%** have scaled voice AI across multiple customer journeys, meaning it's no longer a side project owned by one team.
- **30%** of the organizations that did scale report a clear, positive revenue impact from doing so, and **68%** say they reached ROI within six to twenty-four months.

Read together, those numbers say something specific: the businesses that push through to real scale do see returns, and reasonably quickly. The problem isn't that voice AI doesn't pay off. It's that most pilots never get past the first stage to find out.

The report also found two-thirds of respondents expect their voice AI budgets to rise by up to 50% over the next two years. Indian businesses aren't losing confidence in the technology. They're stuck on execution.

## Three stages, and where most companies get stuck

Strip away the vendor language, and most voice AI programs sit on a simple ladder.

### Stage 1: Experimentation

A single use case, often inbound support for one product line or one region, run as a proof of concept. Success is measured by "it mostly works," not by a baseline comparison. Most organizations that have ever touched voice AI have been through this stage.

### Stage 2: Limited production

The bot now handles real customer interactions, but only for one workflow, one language, or one business unit. This is where 31% of surveyed organizations sit, and where most pilots quietly stall. The system works, so there's no urgency to expand it, and no one owns the job of scaling it further.

### Stage 3: Scaled across journeys

Voice AI runs across multiple customer touchpoints: sales inquiries, service calls, collections, renewals, each with its own tuned flows, and the organization treats it as core infrastructure, not a side project. Only 13% of surveyed organizations have reached this stage, which is why it's the one worth studying.

## Why stage 2 is where pilots die

Three patterns explain most of the stall, based on what the report measured:

**Language coverage stays thin.** Only about a third of organizations running voice AI in production support three or more Indian languages. For a country where customer conversations routinely switch between English, Hindi and a regional language mid-call, a bot that handles only one language caps its own usefulness before it starts.

**Nobody owns the expansion decision.** A pilot that launched inside one team rarely gets a mandate to expand into another team's workflow without a deliberate decision from someone above both teams. Without that decision point built into the plan, limited production becomes the permanent state.

**ROI gets measured too narrowly or not at all.** The report's own numbers show that scaled deployments do produce revenue impact and ROI within a reasonable window. But that's only visible to a company that measured a clear baseline before starting and the same metric after. Many pilots skip that step entirely, so even a genuinely working deployment can't make the case for its own expansion.

## A practical checklist to move from stage 2 to stage 3

1. **Name an owner for scale, not just for the pilot.** The person who ran the first deployment is rarely the person with the mandate to expand it into a second business unit. Decide who that is before you need the decision.
2. **Measure one number before you expand, and the same number after.** Cost per interaction, average handling time or resolution rate, whichever matters most for the workflow you're adding next. Without a before-and-after number, you can't make the case to the next team.
3. **Add languages before you add use cases.** The sectors leading in this report, BFSI, telecom, e-commerce, retail and CPG, travel and logistics, all deal in high call volumes across language-diverse customer bases. If your bot still runs in one language, that's the ceiling to break before anything else.
4. **Pick the second journey deliberately.** Don't let the next use case be whichever team asks loudest. Pick the one with the highest call volume and the most repetitive conversation pattern, the same logic that made your first pilot work.
5. **Revisit the build-versus-partner decision at this stage, not before.** A narrow pilot is often fine to build or run with a single point solution. Scaling across journeys usually needs more engineering depth, especially for routing, escalation and multilingual accuracy, than most internal teams have spare capacity for.
