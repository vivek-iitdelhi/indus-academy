On October 6, ElevenLabs held its first dedicated India summit, in Bengaluru, and used it to make a point I've been making for a while: voice is India's most natural interface for AI, not an afterthought feature on top of a chat product. If you've been treating voice agents as something to revisit "once the technology matures," the announcements from that event are a reason to move that conversation up.

## Why a global voice AI company chose Bengaluru

At the summit, ElevenLabs co-founder and CEO Mati Staniszewski [described India as the company's largest market outside the United States](https://www.dqindia.com/data-and-ai/elevenlabs-pushes-voice-ai-from-sounding-human-to-running-the-business-in-india-12629106), and said businesses here generated 100 million conversations through ElevenAgents, its conversational agent platform, over the past year. The framing of the event itself was telling: the emphasis wasn't on how human a synthetic voice can sound, but on agents that handle real business conversations end to end — claims filing, support calls, appointment booking. That's the shift that matters for you. A voice that sounds convincing in a demo is a novelty; a voice agent that resolves a customer's actual request is a cost center you can automate.

## The language gap is closing, but not yet closed

For Indian businesses, the real blocker has never been whether AI can speak Hindi or Tamil in isolation — it's whether it can do so reliably enough to put in front of a customer, in the specific language and dialect your customers actually use. ElevenLabs currently covers [14 of India's 22 officially recognized languages](https://www.newsbytesapp.com/news/business/elevenlabs-to-expand-voice-ai-to-22-indian-languages/tldr) on its Eleven v4 model, out of more than 90 languages supported globally, and Staniszewski said the rest are coming — though reports on the timeline disagree, with estimates ranging from the coming months to next year.

The practical takeaway: don't take a vendor's "multilingual support" at face value. Before you pilot anything, get the exact list of languages and dialects they currently support in production (not on a roadmap), and test it with a real sample of your customers' actual speech — code-switching, accents and all. A model that handles clean, formal Hindi in a demo often struggles with the Hinglish your call center actually hears.

## A state government is already testing this

The clearest signal that voice AI is moving from pilot to policy in India came separately: the Karnataka government, through the Karnataka Innovation and Technology Society, [signed a non-binding memorandum of understanding with ElevenLabs](https://yourstory.com/2026/10/karnataka-govt-signs-mou-with-elevenlabs-for-voice-ai-services) on the same day as the summit. It covers three areas — a voice AI safety framework (deepfake detection, audio watermarking, consent requirements for voice cloning), a voice-restoration program in Kannada for people who've lost the ability to speak, and exploring Kannada voice AI for citizen services and grievance redressal.

Worth being precise here: this is a non-binding agreement with no procurement commitment or deployment timeline attached — it signals direction, not a finished rollout. But it's still useful to you as a benchmark. If a state government is building a consent and watermarking framework for voice cloning before it deploys anything citizen-facing, that's the minimum bar a business should be setting for itself too, especially if your agents will ever use a cloned or branded voice rather than a stock one.

## What this means if you haven't piloted voice AI yet

None of this means every business should rush into a full voice-agent deployment. It means the excuses for not starting a small pilot are getting weaker. Here's how to run one without overcommitting:

1. **Pick one high-volume, low-complexity call flow.** Order status checks, appointment confirmations and basic FAQ calls are good starting points — the downside of a bad AI answer is low, and the volume is high enough to measure quickly.
2. **Measure the baseline before you automate it.** Know your current average call time, resolution rate and cost per call. Without that, you can't tell if the pilot actually worked.
3. **Test in the specific language mix your customers use**, not the vendor's cleanest demo language. Insist on a trial with your own call recordings if the vendor will allow it.
4. **Decide upfront where a human has to take over.** Anything involving money, a complaint, or a decision that affects the customer's account should hand off to a person, not resolve silently.
5. **Ask about data residency and consent, especially for cloned voices.** If a vendor can clone a voice, ask exactly what consent and storage controls exist before you ever use that capability.
6. **Compare cost per conversation, not cost per minute.** Vendors price differently, and a cheaper per-minute rate can still cost more per resolved call if the agent needs more turns to get there.
