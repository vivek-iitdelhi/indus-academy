Most companies choose an AI vendor the way they choose a courier service: compare the demo, compare the price, sign the contract. Almost nobody asks whether the vendor itself is financially sound enough to still be running the same product, on the same terms, in two years. That question has been nearly impossible to answer for the big AI labs, because none of them have had to disclose anything. That's starting to change. On October 26, Anthropic [filed a registration statement on Form S-1](https://www.anthropic.com/news/s1-sec) with the US Securities and Exchange Commission for a proposed initial public offering, planning to list its Class A common stock on the Nasdaq Global Select Market under the ticker "ANT." If your company has bet any part of its workflow on Claude, OpenAI's models, or any other AI vendor, it's worth understanding what this kind of filing will eventually let you check, and why you shouldn't wait for your vendor to go public to start asking the same questions.

## What Anthropic actually announced

The filing itself is procedural, not dramatic. Anthropic's own announcement confirms the S-1 has been filed but is **not yet effective** — meaning the shares cannot be bought or sold yet. The number of shares and the price range have "not yet been determined." Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley are named as the lead underwriters, listed as the contacts for obtaining the preliminary prospectus once it's available. No valuation, revenue figure or timeline for the actual listing appears in the announcement. Several more steps remain, including setting the offering size and price range and having the SEC declare the registration statement effective, before any trading happens.

It's worth being precise about what this is and isn't. Filing an S-1 is a step toward becoming a public company; it isn't itself a signal that Anthropic needs cash urgently or that anything is wrong. Large, well-funded private companies go public for many reasons — liquidity for early investors and employees, access to public capital markets, or simply the scale at which staying private stops making sense. Don't read "IPO filing" as either a red flag or a reassurance on its own.

## Why a vendor going public actually matters to you

Right now, if your company depends on Anthropic, OpenAI, or any other major AI lab, you have almost no independently verifiable information about that company's financial health. You don't know their revenue concentration (how much of their income comes from a handful of large customers), their cash burn rate, their dependency on a single cloud or chip supplier, or what's in the fine print of their biggest contracts. You're trusting a sales deck and a logo wall.

Once a company is public, US securities law requires a different standard of disclosure. A listed company must file a prospectus, then ongoing quarterly (10-Q) and annual (10-K) reports, which typically include:

- **Revenue growth and customer concentration** — whether the business depends on a small number of very large customers, which matters if you're a mid-sized customer who could get deprioritized.
- **A "risk factors" section**, where companies are legally required to lay out, in detail, what could go wrong with their own business — competitive pressure, regulatory exposure, key supplier dependencies, litigation. This section is usually the most candid document a company ever publishes about itself.
- **Gross margins and cost structure**, which for an AI lab means how exposed they are to the cost of compute, and therefore how likely a price increase is.
- **Related-party and major-shareholder disclosures**, which show you who actually controls strategic decisions.

None of that exists today for Anthropic, OpenAI, or Google's AI division as a standalone business. Once Anthropic's filing becomes effective and it starts reporting as a public company, a meaningful slice of that information becomes available to anyone, for free, on the SEC's EDGAR database — not just to companies big enough to negotiate special disclosure clauses into their contracts.

## How to use this once the filings exist

When Anthropic's prospectus and, eventually, its quarterly filings become public, add them to your AI vendor review the way you'd review a supplier's financials before a large contract:

1. **Check customer concentration in the risk factors section.** If a vendor discloses that a small number of customers make up a large share of revenue, ask where you sit relative to that — you don't want to be a rounding error to a vendor whose roadmap follows its biggest accounts.
2. **Read the competitive and regulatory risk disclosures**, not the marketing language elsewhere in the prospectus. Companies are required to be honest here in a way they're not required to be anywhere else.
3. **Look at compute and supplier dependency.** An AI lab's biggest cost is usually compute, often from one or two cloud or chip partners. A dependency disclosed there is a dependency that can eventually show up in your pricing.
4. **Track this quarterly, not once.** A single filing is a snapshot. The trend across two or three quarters — growing or shrinking customer concentration, changing margins — tells you more than any one number.

## What to do while the filings don't exist yet

Anthropic's filing isn't effective yet, and most AI vendors you use aren't public at all and may never be. That doesn't mean you have to wait or go in blind. Ask your current AI vendors, in writing, for the private-company equivalent of what a filing would tell you: their largest customer as a percentage of revenue (even a range), their primary compute or infrastructure dependency, and what happens to your service if that one dependency fails. A vendor confident in their numbers will usually give you a straight answer or a reasonable reason they can't; a vendor who deflects the question entirely is telling you something too.

## This isn't a reason to distrust Anthropic specifically

It's worth saying plainly: an IPO filing is Anthropic choosing more scrutiny, not less. Public companies answer to regulators, auditors and shareholders in ways private companies don't have to. If anything, this is a reminder that the AI vendor landscape is maturing past the "trust the demo" phase, and your own vendor review process should mature with it — for every AI tool your company depends on, not just the one that happened to file paperwork this week.
