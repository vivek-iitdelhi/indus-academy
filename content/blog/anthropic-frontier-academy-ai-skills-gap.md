Most companies I talk to have the same quiet problem: hundreds of people with a Claude, ChatGPT or Copilot license, and a handful who actually move the needle with it. On October 2, Anthropic [put a number on that gap](https://anthropic.com/news/claude-frontier-academy): $100 million to train 10,000 "Frontier Deployed Engineers" by the end of 2027, through a new program called Claude Frontier Academy. The number is big. The structure underneath it is the more useful thing to study.

## What Anthropic actually announced

Claude Frontier Academy trains engineers nominated by companies already in Anthropic's Claude Partner Network — the first cohorts include people from Accenture, Bain, Capgemini, Commonwealth Bank of Australia, Deloitte, McKinsey, Morgan Stanley and Novo Nordisk. The program runs in two stages, modeled explicitly on medical residency training:

1. **A multi-day in-person program**, where participants work alongside Anthropic engineers and licensed instructors on a simulated enterprise deployment, then sit a graded practical. Passing earns a "Claude Resident Engineer" badge.
2. **A 12-week residency**, where participants lead a real Claude use case back at their own organization, supported by Anthropic engineers and their cohort. Passing a final assessment earns the "Claude Frontier Deployed Engineer" badge. Anthropic expects the first of these around early 2027.

Cohorts are already running in San Francisco, New York and London. Accenture's Ram Ramalingam described the practical assessment as testing "judgment tested against a simulated enterprise deployment, not technical skills in isolation" — which is the detail worth sitting with, whether or not you'll ever touch this specific program.

## Why a residency, not a course

The AI training market is full of courses, certificates and workshops. Anthropic deliberately built something closer to a medical residency: watch, do a graded practical under supervision, then go run a real case with a safety net of mentors, before anyone calls you qualified. That sequence — learn, practice on something real, get assessed, only then operate independently — is a direct answer to a failure mode every L&D leader in India has seen: a batch of employees finishes an AI course, gets a certificate, and changes nothing about how they actually work on Monday morning.

A certificate measures attendance. A graded practical on a real (or realistic) problem measures whether someone can actually do the thing. That distinction, not the brand name on the badge, is what makes a training program worth running.

## The real signal: it's not about everyone

Anthropic's own framing matters here. Steve Corfield, the company's Global Head of Business Development and Partnerships, said the hard part is "building that depth of talent, engineers with the highest level of AI fluency" — and the eligibility bar reflects it. Candidates need to already be hands-on software engineers with strong fundamentals, a record of building with large language models, and experience helping others adopt AI. Prior agent-building experience isn't required, but general technical depth is.

This is a direct, real-world instance of a view I hold strongly: skills beat tools. Anthropic isn't trying to make 10,000 people fluent in Claude specifically. It's trying to concentrate its training budget on a small number of people whose judgment can be trusted on messy, real deployments — and it's explicitly not training everyone, because that's not where the constraint is. For context, the Claude Partner Network already has more than 175,000 Claude certifications across 46,000 firms, and roughly 4,000 people have completed Anthropic's existing Basecamp program. The 10,000-person Frontier Academy target sits on top of that broad base, aimed at a much narrower, deeper tier.

Most Indian companies have this backwards: broad, shallow AI literacy training for everyone, and no equivalent deep bench for the handful of people who should become your internal AI generalists. Both layers matter. But if you can only fund one well this quarter, the data from this program says fund the narrow, deep one.

## The blueprint you can run without $100 million

You don't need Anthropic's budget or partner status to copy the structure. Four things carry over directly:

**1. Pick the few, not the many.** Identify three to five people across your teams who already show unusual judgment with AI tools — not the most enthusiastic users, the ones whose output you'd actually trust unsupervised. Invest disproportionately in them before you invest further in company-wide literacy training.

**2. Make the project real, not a simulation.** Anthropic's residency has people lead an actual use case at their own company, not a generic exercise. Pick one frequent, painful workflow in your business — a report, a reconciliation, a customer response process — and make that the training ground. The stakes of getting it wrong should be real but recoverable.

**3. Grade before you scale.** Build in an actual checkpoint: a working demo, a measured before-and-after, a peer review, before you let someone roll a workflow out to a wider team. This is the step most internal AI pilots skip, and it's the one that catches a plausible-sounding but wrong approach before it spreads.

**4. Pair, don't abandon.** Anthropic's residents keep access to engineers and a cohort for 12 weeks. Your internal equivalent is a named mentor or a small peer group who meet every two weeks to compare what's working. Training that ends the day the workshop does rarely survives contact with a real workload.

## Where this still needs a human decision

None of this replaces broad AI literacy training across your workforce — the kind that helps every analyst, marketer or ops person use AI better in their existing role. It solves a different, narrower problem: who do you trust to design and own an AI-driven workflow that other people will depend on. Confusing the two is how companies end up with AI champions who were simply the first to raise their hand, rather than the people best equipped to get it right.

## The bottom line

Anthropic's $100 million figure is a useful data point: even a company that builds frontier AI models believes the scarce resource is depth of judgment in a small number of people, not broader access to the tool. You can't buy your way into their cohorts, but you can borrow the structure — narrow the group, use a real project, grade it properly, and keep mentorship going past the workshop.
